I build hardware and software that help research teams test their ideas faster.

Right now that’s mostly cooling research, where I’m connecting the rig, the parts we make, and the test data so AI tools can help. Before that I built test benches for carbon capture, and before that I ran a college makerspace.

Currently at Orbital Industries.
Previously at Noya and Laney College.

What I do

Most of my work starts the same way. A team has an idea they can’t test yet, so I figure out what’s missing and build it.

Usually that’s a test rig. I’ll take it from the first layout through the hazard review and commissioning, and then I’m the one fixing it when it breaks. Sometimes it’s a shop or a new machine, so we can make a part in a few days instead of waiting on a supplier.

Lately it’s been more software. Mostly tools that keep track of what’s on a rig, what we tested, and what we decided, so people and AI tools are looking at the same information.

I also write the design guides, training, and procedures, so other people can use what I set up without me.

Work

AI-assisted cooling research

Orbital Industries, 2026

I helped build and improve a test system for two-phase cooling research. A lot of my work is connecting the rig, the parts we make, and the test data, so we can take an idea all the way to a physical test.

I also developed an AI-assisted cold-plate design workflow and took generated lid designs through fabrication and testing.

More about the research

When the program moved to two-phase cooling, I proposed testing one cold plate at a time on a simpler bench. The team went with that, and I led the early design of the rig.

I worked with the team on changes to the rig, test procedures, and the contact surfaces that affect our measurements. Getting those details right helps us understand whether a design change actually improved cooling.

The design workflow used experimental data and basic physics models to propose new lids. We tested the resulting parts, and another engineer built on the workflow. The generated designs hadn’t outperformed our best existing lid at that stage, but we had a working path from an AI-assisted proposal to a physical experiment.

I also ran the hazard reviews for the rig, and wrote our respiratory protection program with help from outside specialists. That gave us documented safeguards and training as we started doing more refrigerant testing.

Making research hardware usable by software

Orbital Industries, 2026

AI tools need a useful record of the system they’re working with. I built tools to capture a rig’s components and connections as structured data and generate engineering diagrams from that record.

More about the engineering tools

The work grew into an engineering workbench built around DEXPI, a standard for exchanging plant engineering data. I worked on keeping the model consistent with the physical rig and giving components stable identities as the design changed.

I also worked on connecting experimental runs to the parts being tested. That context matters when an engineer or an AI tool tries to compare results or propose the next experiment.

I also built tools for running and keeping an eye on the team’s flow simulations, and for picking runs back up when they get interrupted. In one AI-assisted CFD comparison, the overall error score looked fine but some of the trends were wrong. I pointed that out so we knew what the model could and couldn’t be trusted for before we used it to make design decisions.

Getting research parts made

Orbital Industries, 2026

I brought a new milling capability from equipment selection through commissioning and early cold-plate samples, and set up 3D printing too. If a part is a good fit for it, we can go from a design to a physical prototype in days. Sending it out can take weeks or months.

I also work with suppliers on cooling surfaces that need other fabrication methods.

My part is working out what can be made, getting the tooling and drawings ready, and checking the parts before they go into a test.

More about fabrication

Setting up the shop included preparing the space, coordinating training, and writing a design guide. I also built software for tooling records and machine integration.

When I hooked the mill up to our AI tools, I added operator confirmations and machine-state checks. I also spelled out what a software stop command can’t be trusted to do, so a person is still checking things at the machine.

For supplier-made samples, I compare fabrication routes and ask how bonding or finishing might change the surface we’re trying to test. I document inspection methods and their limits so the team knows what we’ve actually checked.

A bot for live meeting notes

Orbital Industries, 2026

I built an automated bot that joins live meetings, captures the audio, and transcribes the conversation as it happens. It separates speech by speaker and uses participant information to help identify who said what.

I connected it to our research tools so the team can turn conversations into decisions, risks, and action items, with the transcript there when we need the context. By late July 2026 it had recorded 185 meetings.

More about the bot

The work spans the meeting bot, live audio capture, streaming transcription, speaker attribution, and saving the result. I also worked on recording consent, reconnecting dropped streams, and preserving partial transcripts when a session is interrupted.

I’ve since been developing separate capture and transcription services, with live transcript APIs, evidence for speaker attribution, and a reliable handoff into the team’s research tools. That service work builds on the original bot.

Test benches and a shop for carbon capture

Noya, 2023–2025

I built thermal-imaging and pressure-drop test rigs, ran a three-week prototype build across four engineering groups, and set up a fabrication shop from scratch.

More about the test benches

I built a test bench that uses three thermal cameras to watch a part as it heats. A Python application controls the temperature, records the images, and combines them into a temperature map.

Testing went from roughly 30–40 minutes per part to 5–10 minutes, and engineers no longer had to watch each run. We had about 1,000 parts to get through, so that added up.

The pressure-drop fixture clamps a part with a load cell, so I know it’s sealed the same way every time. Then it pushes up to 500 SLPM of air through the part and logs pressure, temperature, and humidity. To check it, I ran a pipe with a known diameter and length, and compared results with another lab.

More about the build and the shop

For the prototype build, I ran daily stand-ups with controls, process, mechanical, and electrical. I kept track of the schedule and what was blocked, and let leadership know early if something looked at risk. We hit the three-week target.

For the shop, I asked each team what they needed, laid out a 2,000 sq ft space in CAD, found the main tools, and wrote the procedures and training. Parts we used to wait weeks for, we could make the same day.
